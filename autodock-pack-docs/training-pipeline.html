Training the model to know Indian groceries, not just "objects"

You're right that this is the hard part — and the part a generic vision model can't do out of the box. Here's how to actually build it: what to collect, how to train it so new SKUs never need retraining, and a realistic order to tackle rice, dal grades, masalas, packaged goods, and produce.

00

Onboarding a SKU vs. training the model — not the same step

Worth separating these clearly, because the plan changes depending on which one you mean:

  • Onboarding (4 photos, 30 seconds) — adds a new reference fingerprint to the catalog, using a model that is already trained. This is cheap and is what happens every time a store stocks a new item.
  • Training (what this page is about) — teaches the underlying model how to tell Indian groceries apart in the first place: that Basmati and Sona Masoori are different, that broken-grain toor dal looks different from whole-grain, that a green chili and a cucumber are not the same thing at a glance. You do this once (and periodically improve it), on a proper dataset — not per SKU.

Get training right and onboarding stays cheap forever. Get it wrong and every new SKU becomes a retraining project — exactly what your "no retraining" pitch is trying to avoid.

01

Not all products are equally hard — tier them

"All Indian products" is really three very different computer-vision problems wearing one trenchcoat. Treat them separately and the hard 20% stops eating budget meant for the easy 80%.

Difficulty tiers and what each one actually needs
TierExamplesWhat makes it hardWhat it needs
Tier 1Branded packets — biscuits, snacks, spice pouches, bottled goodsBarely hard — distinct box art, logos, colorsStandard classification; barcode/weight can do most of the confirming
Tier 2Rice varieties, dal types & quality grades, whole/powdered masalasVisually near-identical between variants; grades differ subtlyFine-grained fingerprint model + weight/density fusion — your actual moat
Tier 3Fruits & vegetablesNo fixed shape — size, ripeness and color shift dailyCategory-level recognition (not exact variety) + weight-driven pricing
Why this mattersTier 1 is where a lot of your SKU count lives but almost none of your risk. Tier 2 is where the device either earns its "unique" claim or doesn't. Tier 3 is a different problem again — don't try to solve it with the same approach as Tier 2.
02

Collecting the training data, tier by tier

Tier 1 Packaged goods

  1. Reuse the 4-photo onboarding flowit's already enough signal — distinct packaging does most of the work.
  2. Scale it by crowdsourcing across brancheswhoever restocks a new packaged item photographs it on the spot; no dedicated data-collection trip needed.

Tier 2 Rice, dal, masalas — the fine-grained set

  1. Build one fixed capture rigsame lighting, same camera angle and distance you'll use in the actual kiosk tray — consistency here matters more than volume.
  2. Source real graded samples, not just "a bag of dal"get physical samples of each quality grade from your supplier or a wholesale mandi contact — you cannot photograph a quality difference you don't have in hand.
  3. Shoot 100–300 images per variety/gradevary fill level (empty tray → full), natural lighting drift across the day, and loose-pile arrangement.
  4. Log the density number alongside every shotweight ÷ estimated tray-fill volume — this is training data for the numeric feature, not just the image.

Tier 3 Fruits & vegetables

  1. Capture across a freshness timelinethe same batch photographed on day 1, 3 and 5 — produce genuinely looks different as it ages, and the model needs to see that range.
  2. Shoot multiple specimens, not one "reference" item30–50 images per type across natural size and ripeness variation — a single perfect photo of one tomato won't generalize.
03

Don't start from zero

Two different kinds of head start, for two different tiers:

  • The backbone itself: start from an ImageNet-pretrained MobileNetV4 (or similar), not a randomly initialized network. You're fine-tuning an existing visual understanding, not teaching a model to see from scratch — that's what makes a few hundred images per category realistic instead of needing millions.
  • Produce specifically: public datasets exist (Fruits-360, VegFru, and similar) and can pretrain the produce-recognition head before you fine-tune on your own photos. They skew Western, though — okra, drumstick, bitter gourd and other India-specific produce will need your own collection regardless.
  • Rice/dal/masala varieties and quality grades: there isn't a strong public dataset for this — which is exactly why it's a real moat. No shortcut here; this is the collection work in Section 02 that only you're doing.
04

Train for "how similar," not "which class"

The training method is what actually delivers your "no retraining, 30-second onboarding" promise — and it's not a standard image classifier. A classifier bakes in a fixed list of SKUs; add one and you have to retrain. Instead, fine-tune the embedding model with metric learning (triplet or contrastive loss): it learns a general sense of "how visually similar are these two items," and a new SKU is just a new point in that space — no retraining, ever.

  1. Build training tripletsanchor photo, a positive (same SKU/variety), and a negative (a different one).
  2. Make the negatives hard on purposeexplicitly pair Basmati against Sona Masoori, whole toor dal against broken toor dal — the confusions you actually care about, not random unrelated items.
  3. Fine-tune the backbone against this triplet objectiveon the labeled Tier 2/3 dataset from Section 02.
  4. Re-populate the FAISS reference index from the new embeddingsthis is the only "onboarding-side" step that changes — the model itself is done.
05

The honest hard part: dal quality grades

Telling toor dal from moong dal is a variety problem — hard, but solvable with the approach above. Telling premium toor dal from standard toor dal is a grading problem, and it's genuinely harder: the visual differences (broken-grain %, color uniformity, dust) are subtler than variety differences, and a wide top-down tray shot may not resolve them well.

RecommendationDon't ask the camera to certify grain quality from a checkout-distance photo alone. Instead, tie the quality grade to which bin or hopper the store loaded — when staff restock, they declare "this hopper is Grade A toor dal" once; vision only needs to confirm "this is toor dal" (a variety problem it's good at), and the system trusts the declared grade for whatever's currently in that hopper. Treat pure vision-based grading as a stretch goal to chase after launch, not a launch requirement.
06

Where the training actually runs

  1. Now — Mac M4 via PyTorch + MPSyour datasets are hundreds to low-thousands of images per category; M4 unified memory handles this fine, no cloud GPU needed yet.
  2. Later — a rented cloud GPU (A10/A100-class)once real pilot data pushes your dataset into the tens of thousands of images, for faster iteration. Not needed for the initial build.
  3. Always — re-validate accuracy after RKNN INT8 quantizationa model that's 96% accurate in PyTorch on the M4 is not guaranteed to still be 96% accurate after quantization on the real RK3588 board. Check both, every time, per the Track B gate in the build roadmap.
07

What "good enough" looks like, per tier

Target accuracy before calling a tier launch-ready
TierTargetFallback for the rest
Tier 1 Packaged≥99% top-1Should rarely be needed — packaging is distinctive
Tier 2 Variety (rice/dal/masala)≥95% top-1Show top-2 candidates on the touchscreen for a 1-tap confirm
Tier 2 Quality grade≥85% (or hopper-declared)Defer to declared hopper grade, per Section 05
Tier 3 Produce≥97% category-levelTouchscreen confirm for the ambiguous tail
Why a touchscreen fallback, not 100% accuracyForcing every tier to near-100% before launch is how this project stalls. A low-confidence item showing its top two guesses for a one-tap confirm keeps the customer moving in under a second — genuinely faster than chasing the last few points of model accuracy, and it's what grab-and-go systems elsewhere do for exactly this reason.
08

The loop that gets you the rest of the way

You will not pre-collect every Indian grocery item before launch, and you don't need to — the model keeps learning from real store use if you wire the feedback path in from day one.

Practically: tag every transaction as auto-confirmed or customer-corrected in the same event log your analytics already use. A corrected item is a free, real-world labeled example — periodically (monthly is reasonable to start) pull those into the next fine-tuning run.

09

A realistic order to actually do this in

"All Indian products" as a day-one training target would stall the pilot for months. Scope it in waves instead — the loop in Section 08 is what gets you to full coverage, not a single upfront data-collection marathon.

Suggested phasing
PhaseCoversWhen
1Top 50–80 SKUs by expected sales volume, across all three tiers — enough for a believable pilotAlongside Track A/B of the build roadmap
2Full rice/dal/masala variety + grade set you actually stockBefore scaling past the first pilot store
3Everything else, via the feedback loop aboveContinuous, post-launch

Pick the Phase 1 list with whoever owns merchandising — the 50–80 items customers actually buy most, deliberately including a couple of your hardest lookalike pairs (a rice variety pair, a dal grade pair) so the pilot proves the hard case, not just the easy 80%.
